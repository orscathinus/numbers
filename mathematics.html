Classification wall

What kind of number is 1?

These labels overlap. A single number can inhabit many mathematical sets at once.

ℕ

Natural

Under the common convention that positive counting numbers begin at 1, it is the first natural number.

ℤ

Integer

It has no fractional part and lies immediately to the right of 0 on the integer number line.

ℚ

Rational

1 = 1/1, so it is a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator.

ℝ

Real

It lies on the real number line and is also the endpoint of the unit interval [0,1].

ℂ

Complex

Every real number is complex: 1 can be written 1 + 0i.

x²

Perfect square

1 = 1² (and also (−1)²), making 1 the smallest positive square.

2k+1

Odd

1 = 2·0 + 1, so it fits the standard form for an odd integer.

∣

One divisor

Its only positive divisor is 1 itself.

≠ p,c

Neither prime nor composite

Modern definitions reserve prime and composite for integers greater than 1.

Why 1 is the multiplicative identity

An identity element is one that leaves every element unchanged under an operation. For multiplication, that element is 1: a·1 = 1·a = a. This pattern is built directly into familiar fields such as the rational, real, and complex numbers.

Why 1 is not prime

A positive integer is prime when it is greater than 1 and has exactly two positive divisors, 1 and itself. Because 1 has only one positive divisor, it does not qualify. The convention also makes unique prime factorization clean: 12 = 2²·3 has one prime factorization apart from order, rather than endlessly many versions padded with factors of 1.

Factorials and empty products

By definition, 1! = 1. A deeper echo appears at 0! = 1: products with no factors are conventionally assigned the multiplicative identity, just as sums with no terms are assigned the additive identity 0. This convention keeps many formulas in combinatorics and algebra uniform.

Powers and reciprocals

For every integer exponent n, 1ⁿ = 1. The reciprocal of 1 is also 1, making it self-reciprocal. In complex analysis and algebra, the equation zⁿ = 1 produces the nth roots of unity; 1 is always one of them.

Number-theoretic functions at 1

Several arithmetic functions take their identity-friendly base value at 1: the divisor-counting function τ(1)=1, the sum-of-divisors function σ(1)=1, and Euler’s totient φ(1)=1 under the standard convention. These boundary values help formulas behave uniformly.

Museum note

“First” needs a convention

Some authors include 0 in the natural numbers and some begin them at 1. So the safest universal statement is that 1 is the first positive integer.

A tiny fixed point
1ⁿ = 1

For every integer n, including negative n, exponentiation leaves 1 unchanged.
