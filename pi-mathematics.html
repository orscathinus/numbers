π beyond
the circle.

The circle definition is only the entrance. Inside: radians, integrals, infinite series and products, complex exponentials, zeta values, probability, and the distinction between irrational and transcendental.

Core identities

One constant, many doors.

These formulas are not definitions of unrelated objects. They are equivalent appearances of the same constant in geometry, analysis, trigonometry, and complex arithmetic.

Because arctan(1)=π/4.

eiπ + 1 = 0

Euler's identity connects π with e, i, 1, and 0 through the complex exponential.

Euler's Basel-problem result links π to the sum of reciprocal squares.

Radians make π the natural angular unit

An angle in radians is arc length divided by radius. Therefore a full turn is 2π radians because the full circumference is 2πr. The derivative formulas d(sin x)/dx=cos x and d(cos x)/dx=−sin x take their cleanest form when x is measured in radians.

Archimedean squeezing

Inscribed polygons have perimeters below a circle's circumference and circumscribed polygons have perimeters above it. By increasing the number of sides, the two bounds can be made increasingly tight. Archimedes used 96-sided polygons to prove 3+10/71 < π < 3+1/7. This is an early rigorous bounding argument rather than mere decimal guessing.

Infinite series

The Gregory–Leibniz series π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + ⋯ is conceptually beautiful but converges painfully slowly. Faster formulas—especially arctangent identities and modern algorithms—are far better for computation. The slow series is useful pedagogically because every partial sum visibly alternates around π/4.

Infinite products

Wallis's product expresses π/2 as an infinite product of rational factors: (2/1)(2/3)(4/3)(4/5)(6/5)(6/7)⋯. It is another striking example of π arising from limits rather than a direct circle measurement.

Irrational versus transcendental

Irrational means “not equal to a ratio of integers.” Transcendental means “not algebraic,” so it is not a root of any nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients. Every transcendental real number is irrational, but not every irrational number is transcendental: √2, for instance, is irrational but algebraic.

π in even zeta values

The Riemann zeta function satisfies ζ(2)=π²/6, ζ(4)=π⁴/90, and analogous formulas for every positive even integer argument. These identities are part of a broad analytic structure involving Bernoulli numbers.

Do the digits contain everything?

Computations suggest highly even-looking digit frequencies, but no proof is known that π is normal in base 10 or any other integer base. Irrationality alone guarantees only nontermination and nonperiodicity—not that every possible finite string occurs, much less with equal limiting frequency.

Classification

π lives in ℝ

  • Transcendental
  • Not rational
  • Not algebraic
  • Not an integer or natural number
