History Orbit

Thousands of years
closing the circle.

π was measured, bounded, approximated, symbolized, proved irrational, proved transcendental, and eventually computed by machines to scales unimaginable to its earliest investigators.

Approximation timeline

π before π.

The constant existed mathematically long before anyone denoted it with the Greek letter π. Early cultures used practical approximations; later mathematicians developed systematic geometric and analytic methods.

c. 1650 BCE

Rhind Mathematical Papyrus

An Egyptian rule corresponds to a value near 3.1605. It is evidence of a practical area rule, not the modern symbolic theory of π.

3rd c. BCE

Archimedes

Using inscribed and circumscribed polygons, Archimedes rigorously bounded π between 223/71 and 22/7. His method made approximation a proof-driven process.

263 CE

Liu Hui

Chinese mathematician Liu Hui used polygonal methods to obtain increasingly accurate approximations, including 3.14159 in later calculations associated with his method.

5th c.

Zu Chongzhi

Zu Chongzhi gave the remarkable fraction 355/113, accurate to six decimal places. It remained an exceptionally strong rational approximation for centuries.

c. 1424

Jamshīd al-Kāshī

Al-Kāshī computed π to about 16 decimal digits using polygonal techniques of great numerical sophistication.

1706

William Jones uses π

Welsh mathematician William Jones used π with essentially its modern meaning for the circumference-to-diameter ratio. Euler's later adoption helped standardize it.

1760s

Lambert proves irrationality

Johann Heinrich Lambert proved π irrational, establishing that no exact fraction p/q equals it.

1882

Lindemann proves transcendence

Ferdinand von Lindemann proved π transcendental. As a consequence, classical squaring of the circle with straightedge and compass is impossible.

1949 onward

Electronic computation

Early electronic computers pushed π into thousands of digits; later algorithms and hardware expanded the scale enormously. Computing π became a benchmark for algorithms and systems as well as a mathematical pursuit.

22/7 was never the exact answer

Archimedes' upper bound 22/7 is famous because it is easy to remember, but he did not claim π equals 22/7. His result was an inequality. Likewise, 355/113 is astonishingly accurate but still rational, so it cannot equal the irrational number π.

The symbol arrived late

The Greek letter π is tied to words associated with perimeter or circumference, but notation developed gradually. William Jones's 1706 use is generally credited as the first use of π for the modern circumference-to-diameter constant; Leonhard Euler's influence later made it standard.

Proof changed the question

For millennia the visible challenge was “how accurately can we approximate the circle ratio?” Irrationality changed the conceptual landscape: there is no terminating or repeating exact decimal or exact rational fraction. Transcendence went further and settled an ancient construction problem by showing that π cannot arise from the algebraic operations available to classical straightedge-and-compass constructions.

Digits are not the whole story

Modern record-setting calculations can produce extraordinary numbers of digits, but mathematical understanding of π does not reduce to digit count. Formulas, bounds, proofs of irrationality and transcendence, and connections to analysis often reveal more structure than another block of decimal places.

Curator note

History ≠ legend

Dates and priority claims around ancient mathematics can be uncertain. This room favors documented approximations and named works over dramatic origin stories, and it distinguishes a numerical approximation from a proof about π itself.
