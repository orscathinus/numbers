First encounter

The number that makes a triangle.

One is a unit. Two makes a pair. Three is the first count large enough to enclose a region with straight sides—and the first odd prime.

Prime, but unlike 2

3 is prime: its only positive divisors are 1 and 3. It is the second prime number and the first odd prime. From this point onward every prime is odd except 2, because any even integer greater than 2 is divisible by 2.

Three also has a curious one-line characterization: 1 + 2 = 3. It is the only integer equal to the sum of all preceding positive integers. MathWorld also notes the factorial analogue, 1! + 2! = 3.

The first triangular number after 1

Arrange one dot in a first row and two dots beneath it. The result is a triangle of three dots. In the standard triangular-number sequence, T₂ = 1 + 2 = 3. This is the first nontrivial example of a figurate number built from a geometric arrangement of points.

The connection between three and triangles is deeper than a visual pun. A triangle is the simplest polygon: it has three vertices, three sides, and three interior angles. An equilateral triangle has threefold rotational symmetry; a rotation through 120° sends it onto itself.

Three arithmetic worlds at once

In decimal notation, 3 is written “3.” In binary it is 11₂. In ternary—the base-3 system—it is 10₃. Ternary uses three digits, 0, 1, and 2. A string of n ternary digits therefore has 3ⁿ possible patterns, just as n binary digits have 2ⁿ.

There is also balanced ternary, which uses place values that are powers of 3 but digits conventionally written −1, 0, and +1. That representation makes positive and negative quantities unusually symmetric.

The gateway to cubes

Raising a quantity to the third power is called cubing: x³. The word comes from geometry because a cube with side length x has volume x³. Moving from squares to cubes is a conceptual move from area to volume, and 3 becomes the exponent associated with ordinary three-dimensional volume scaling.

Three roots around a circle

The equation z³ = 1 has three complex solutions: 1 and two non-real cube roots of unity. On the complex plane they sit equally spaced on the unit circle at angles 0°, 120°, and 240°, forming an equilateral triangle. This is one of the cleanest places where arithmetic, algebra, complex numbers, and geometry collapse into the same picture.

One third repeats

The fraction 1/3 has the decimal expansion 0.333…. The repeating digit is not a coincidence: in base 10, division by 3 produces a repeating expansion because 3 does not divide any power of 10. The familiar identity 0.999… = 1 follows immediately by multiplying 1/3 = 0.333… by 3.

Three beyond pure mathematics

Triplets appear in many scientific models. Genetic information is read in three-nucleotide codons. Ordinary Euclidean space is described with three coordinates. Common RGB color systems use three additive channels. And gravitational dynamics become dramatically richer when a third body is added to the classical two-body problem.
