Why this exhibit is special

A finite definition. An infinite number.

A circle gives π an elementary geometric definition, but the resulting constant cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers and is not the root of any nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients.

Geometry

Every Euclidean circle shares one ratio

If a circle has circumference C and diameter d, then C/d is π. Scaling the circle changes both lengths by the same factor, leaving their ratio unchanged. From that same constant come C=2πr and A=πr².

Analysis

π can be defined without drawing a circle

For example, π = 4∫₀¹ 1/(1+x²) dx. It also governs the period of sine and cosine when angles are measured in radians and appears in identities involving exponential and complex functions.

Arithmetic status

Irrational—and transcendental

Its decimal expansion never terminates or becomes periodic. Transcendence is stronger: π satisfies no nonzero polynomial equation with integer coefficients. That fact ultimately proves the classical straightedge-and-compass problem of squaring the circle impossible.

∞

The circle constant

In ordinary Euclidean geometry, the circumference of a circle is proportional to its diameter. The constant of proportionality is π. If the radius is r, then the diameter is 2r and the circumference is 2πr. The area enclosed by the circle is πr². These formulas are not disconnected appearances: the same geometric structure that fixes the boundary ratio also fixes the area scale.

Why the digits never settle

An irrational number cannot be written as p/q for integers p and nonzero q. π is irrational, so its decimal expansion is infinite and nonrepeating. But “the digits look random” is not a proof of irrationality, and irrationality does not imply that the digits are statistically random. Whether π is normal in base 10—meaning every finite digit string occurs with the expected limiting frequency—is not known.

Transcendence goes further

An algebraic number is a root of some nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients. π is not algebraic: it is transcendental. Ferdinand von Lindemann proved this in 1882. Because a constructible length obtained by straightedge and compass must be algebraic, π's transcendence implies that a square with exactly the same area as a given circle cannot in general be constructed by those classical tools.

Radians put π into motion

Radians define angle by arc length divided by radius. A full circumference has arc length 2πr, so one full turn measures 2π radians; a half-turn is π, a quarter-turn π/2. This is why π appears naturally in calculus and differential equations involving periodic motion rather than being an arbitrary conversion factor.
