Four,
squared.

The first composite number is also the first even square, the first repeated prime power, and the smallest order at which group structure branches into two genuinely different possibilities.

Classification wall

What kind of number is 4?

Four sits at an unusually busy intersection of elementary number theory, algebra, and geometry.

2²

Prime power

4 is 2 squared. Its prime factorization contains one prime, 2, with exponent 2.

p·p

Composite

It has a nontrivial factorization 2×2, so it is the smallest positive composite integer.

□

Perfect square

4 = 2², making it the first even perfect square and the second positive square after 1.

2k

Even

4 = 2·2, so it lies in residue class 0 modulo 2.

τ=3

Three divisors

The positive divisors are exactly 1, 2, and 4. Thus τ(4)=3.

μ=0

Not square-free

The Möbius function μ(4)=0 because 4 is divisible by the square of a prime.

10₄

Quaternary base

In base 4, the decimal number four is written 10. The available digits are 0,1,2,3.

x⁴=1

Four roots of unity

The solutions are 1, i, −1, and −i, equally spaced around the complex unit circle.

C₄ ≠ V₄

Two group types

There are two groups of order 4 up to isomorphism: cyclic C₄ and the Klein four-group C₂×C₂.

Why 4 is the first composite number

A positive integer greater than 1 is composite if it can be written as a product of two smaller positive integers greater than 1. Two and 3 are prime. Four is 2×2, so the sequence of composite positive integers begins at 4.

The factorization 4=2² is unique up to ordering in the sense of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. It is also the first example of a prime appearing with exponent greater than 1.

Divisor functions at 4

From its factorization 4=2², the divisor-count formula gives τ(4)=2+1=3. Its divisor sum is σ(4)=1+2+4=7. Euler's totient is φ(4)=2: among the integers 1 through 4, exactly 1 and 3 are coprime to 4. The Möbius value μ(4)=0 because a squared prime divides 4.

A divisibility rule hiding in decimal notation

A decimal integer is divisible by 4 exactly when its final two decimal digits form a number divisible by 4. The reason is structural: every place value from the hundreds place upward contains a factor 100, and 100=25×4. Therefore the earlier digits contribute a multiple of 4 and cannot change the remainder modulo 4.

For example, 73,516 is divisible by 4 because 16 is divisible by 4. The rule belongs to base 10; it works because 10² is divisible by 4.

Arithmetic modulo 4

Every integer has remainder 0, 1, 2, or 3 upon division by 4. These four residue classes form the ring ℤ/4ℤ. Its additive structure is cyclic of order 4, but multiplication reveals zero divisors: 2·2 ≡ 0 (mod 4), even though 2 is not congruent to 0. That is one reason arithmetic modulo a composite number behaves differently from arithmetic modulo a prime.

Two different groups of order four

The cyclic group C₄ contains an element of order 4: repeatedly applying one quarter-turn visits all four elements before returning to the identity. The Klein four-group V₄=C₂×C₂ has no element of order 4; every nonidentity element has order 2. They have the same number of elements but are not isomorphic.

The fourth roots of unity

The equation z⁴=1 factors as (z−1)(z+1)(z²+1)=0, producing four complex roots: 1, −1, i, and −i. On the unit circle they occur at arguments 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°. Multiplication by i cycles through them: 1→i→−1→−i→1.

Lagrange's four-square theorem

Every nonnegative integer can be written as a sum of four integer squares; equivalently, every positive integer is a sum of at most four positive-or-zero squares. Examples include 7=2²+1²+1²+1² and 23=3²+3²+2²+1². The first published proof was given by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1770.

Four is genuinely needed in the theorem: not every integer is a sum of three squares. Legendre's three-square theorem characterizes the obstruction, but Lagrange's theorem guarantees that allowing a fourth square removes it.

Quartic equations: the last general radical formula

A general polynomial equation of degree 4 is called a quartic. Quartic equations can be solved by radicals, just as quadratic and cubic equations can. Degree 5 changes the landscape: there is no formula by radicals that solves every general quintic polynomial. This statement is about general polynomial equations, not about whether particular higher-degree equations happen to have radical solutions.
