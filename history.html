Timeline

The long road to the modern glyph

The exact shapes varied by place and manuscript tradition. What survives is a branching history rather than a perfectly straight line.

Prehistory and antiquity

One as a mark

A single notch, stroke, pebble, or token is one of the simplest possible ways to record one item. Tally-like representation is much older than the modern numeral.

3rd century BCE · Indian subcontinent

Brahmi numerals are attested

Brahmi numerals, direct graphic ancestors of later Hindu–Arabic numerals, are attested from the third century BCE. Early forms for the first few numbers could be simple collections of strokes.

1st millennium CE

Indian numeral forms diversify

Brahmi-derived forms developed through regional scripts, including Gupta and later Nagari traditions. The positional decimal system and its glyphs matured over time; the history of the notation system is not the same as the history of any one symbol.

8th–9th centuries

Knowledge moves through the Islamic world

Indian methods of calculation and numerals were studied and transmitted by scholars writing in Arabic. Works associated with al-Khwarizmi and al-Kindi helped document and spread Hindu calculation methods.

Late medieval Europe

Hindu–Arabic numerals spread in Europe

European use grew gradually through trade, scholarship, calculation manuals, and later printing. The written forms continued changing before converging toward familiar modern shapes.

Modern typography

1 becomes a family of shapes

Even today, “1” is not one fixed drawing: fonts may add a top hook, a bottom serif, both, or neither. Handwriting often adds distinguishing strokes to prevent confusion with letters such as I or l.

Curator's caution
one ≠ “1”

The abstract number is independent of the glyph used to write it. Treating the symbol’s history as the number’s origin confuses notation with mathematics.
