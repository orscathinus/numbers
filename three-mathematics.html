What kind of number is 3?

The same object belongs to many overlapping mathematical families.

ℕ

Natural number

Three is a positive counting number and the third positive integer.

p

Prime

Its only positive divisors are 1 and 3. It is the second prime and the first odd prime.

3 = 2·1 + 1, so division by 2 leaves remainder 1.

3 = 2²−1, the first Mersenne prime.

Up to isomorphism, there is one group of order 3; it is cyclic and Abelian.

The first odd prime

A prime number is an integer greater than 1 with exactly two positive divisors. For 3 those divisors are 1 and 3. The prime sequence begins 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …, so 3 is the second prime. It is the first odd prime because 2 is even and every later even integer has 2 as a proper divisor.

The pair 2 and 3 are also the only two primes that are consecutive integers. Any pair of consecutive integers after 2 includes an even integer greater than 2, which cannot be prime.

An integer equal to everything before it

Three satisfies the striking identity 3 = 1 + 2. No later positive integer equals the sum of all earlier positive integers: for n ≥ 4, the sum 1+2+⋯+(n−1)=n(n−1)/2 is already larger than n. Three also satisfies the factorial curiosity 3 = 1! + 2!.

Why 3 is triangular

The nth triangular number is Tₙ = n(n+1)/2. Setting n=2 gives T₂ = 2·3/2 = 3. Geometrically, one dot on the first row and two on the second make a triangular array. This connects arithmetic addition to a geometric figure.

Residues modulo 3

Every integer belongs to exactly one of three congruence classes modulo 3: 0, 1, or 2 mod 3. Equivalently, any integer n can be written uniquely in one of the forms 3q, 3q+1, or 3q+2. This three-way partition is the arithmetic backbone of divisibility by 3 and of the cyclic group ℤ/3ℤ under addition.

Why the digit-sum test works

In decimal notation, 10 ≡ 1 (mod 3). Therefore 10² ≡ 1, 10³ ≡ 1, and every power of 10 is congruent to 1 modulo 3. If a decimal integer is a₀ + 10a₁ + 10²a₂ + ⋯, then modulo 3 it is congruent to a₀ + a₁ + a₂ + ⋯. So an integer is divisible by 3 exactly when its decimal digit sum is divisible by 3.

For example, 372 has digit sum 3+7+2=12; 12 is divisible by 3, so 372 is divisible by 3. The laboratory lets you test this rule on arbitrarily long digit strings.

Arithmetic functions at 3

Because 3 is prime, several standard number-theoretic functions take simple values. The divisor-counting function is τ(3)=2. The sum-of-divisors function is σ(3)=1+3=4. Euler's totient is φ(3)=2, since 1 and 2 are the positive integers up to 3 that are coprime to 3. The Möbius function is μ(3)=−1 because 3 is the product of one distinct prime.

Ternary: arithmetic in base 3

Base 3 uses the digits 0, 1, and 2. Place values are powers of three: …, 27, 9, 3, 1. Thus decimal 3 is 10₃, 8 is 22₃, 9 is 100₃, and 27 is 1000₃. A string of n ternary digits has 3ⁿ possible strings.

Balanced ternary replaces the ordinary digits 0,1,2 with digits representing −1,0,+1, often written −, 0, +. Because place values are still powers of 3, every integer can be represented while positive and negative values are treated symmetrically.

Cubing and cube roots

To cube x is to compute x³. For a real number y, a cube root is a real x satisfying x³=y; unlike even roots, every real number has a real cube root, including negative numbers. Over the complex numbers, every nonzero complex number has three distinct cube roots.

The cube roots of unity

Solving z³=1 gives three roots:

1, −1/2 + (√3/2)i, and −1/2 − (√3/2)i.

Each has absolute value 1. Their arguments differ by 120°, so the three points are equally spaced on the unit circle and form an equilateral triangle. Their sum is 0. The two non-real roots satisfy z²+z+1=0.

C₃: the algebra of a three-step cycle

The cyclic group C₃ has three elements: identity, one step, and two steps. Applying the one-step generator three times returns to the identity. A physical 120° rotation of an equilateral triangle provides a concrete model: rotate by 0°, 120°, or 240°. Under composition these three rotations form a cyclic group of order 3.
